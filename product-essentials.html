The problem

The box arrives before the product does.

  • You spent months on the product. The customer’s first physical contact with any of it is a carton that came from whoever quoted lowest.
  • The label peels at the corner in a warehouse in July.
  • The second print run of the same sticker is a slightly deeper green than the first, and now half your stock looks like a different batch.
  • You wanted 200 boxes. The printer’s minimum was 2,000, so you have 1,800 boxes in a storeroom and a design you would now change.

None of that is a failure of taste. It is what happens when packaging is bought as a commodity from someone who never asked what it was for.

Most printers price packaging by the unit, which means they are structurally motivated to push your order size up and your specification down. Neither of those is in your interest at 200 units.

Why the cheapest quote costs more here

With packaging, a bad run is inventory.

With a brochure, a bad print run is embarrassing. With packaging, a bad run is stock you cannot sell.

If the adhesive is wrong, you find out in a warehouse six weeks later with the whole batch already labelled. If the board is under-specified, you find out when the first carton arrives crushed. Neither of those is fixable with a reprint, because the product is already inside it.

That is why a cheap quote on packaging is a different kind of risk from a cheap quote on stationery. The failure is not the print. The failure is everything the print was wrapped around.

We would rather specify the board correctly and lose the quote than win it and have you find out in July.
